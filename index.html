What the columns mean

FitFirmographic score out of 100 — how closely the company matches the profile CTD sells to. Above 85 is top tier.
AccessStrength of your best warm path in. Four bars means someone in your network knows a target well and has spoken to them recently.
SignalWhat changed at the account most recently. Dancing means active back-and-forth, Re-triggered means a dormant account has new activity, Warm watch means monitoring without outreach.
FunnelWhere the deal sits in your own process.
Work statusWhere CTD's outreach stands, not where the account stands. In progress, on hold, or cancelled.
StrategyWhether the agent has finished generating a plan for this account.
RankComposite priority — fit combined with signal strength and how reachable the account is. Use it to decide what to open first.
ReachesHow many of your accounts this person can get you into. They have a path to at least one person in the buying group.
Strongest linkThe person they know best across those accounts, and how well they know them.
Asks in flightIntro requests your team has sent them that they have not yet acted on. The bar fills as the load grows.
Intros madeCompleted introductions this quarter. A high number with low asks in flight means a reliable connector.
Last askedWhen anyone on your team last sent this person a request. Useful for spacing asks out.
ReadyHas capacity and responds. Safe to ask.
Over-askedMore than four open asks, or three in the last thirty days. Give them a rest before asking again.
Gone quietAsked more than fourteen days ago with no response. Nudge or route around them.

RelationshipHow well the connector knows this person. Strong means recent two-way contact, Familiar means a real but older relationship, Weak means a connection with no history, Unknown means no path at all.
Best pathThe single connector most likely to get you in. Every path is you to one connector to the target — CTD only sends second-degree intros.
PathsHow many different people could reach this target. More paths means more resilience if your first choice declines.

AuthorThe SDR or AE who found the path and wrote the messages. Their part ends when the draft is handed over.
RequestorThe person who actually makes the ask. Usually more senior than the author, and chosen because they have the relationship and the clout for the sender to say yes. The ask goes out under their name.

How to read this prototype

Click a row in Accounts to open Kestrel Dynamics. Inside the account, expand a person on People to see the path explorer, and switch History between Timeline, By rep and Notes & docs. Evidence drawers and if/then rules on Brief all open.

1 · Too many views

Nine left-nav items per account, five tabs inside Strategy, three sub-tabs inside those, plus separate tab rows on Activities, Network Score and Docs — roughly 22 panes, three levels deep, to read one account.

The three tabs answer the questions an AE asks in order: should I work this and what do I do (Brief) · who do I go to and how do I reach them (People) · what has already happened (History).

2 · Redundant information

The same narrative was written out seven times — Overview Verdict, Overview Executive Summary, Strategy Summary (Goal/How/Verdict, with Verdict a verbatim duplicate), Strategy Diagnosis, Activities Executive Summary, Propensity Rep Summary, Firmographic Summary. The Meridian Grid acquisition appears in 7 places, Elena's webinar in 7.

Each fact is now stated once at the level of detail it deserves: headline in The call, one sentence in Why, full detail behind a drawer.

3 · Four competing scores

Firmographic 97, Propensity 37, Network Score "Strong / 196 / 46 / 23%", Rank 9820 — four scales, four screens. Replaced with one read (The call) supported by exactly three components: Fit, Timing, Access. The same three become sortable columns on the Accounts list, so the list and the detail page finally agree.

4 · Typography

Measured on the live Overview page:

  • 15 distinct font sizes — 9, 10, 10.5, 11, 11.5, 12, 12.5, 13, 13.33, 13.5, 14, 15, 16, 17, 22px, including fractional sizes from an unintended % cascade.
  • Three font families on the Tasks grid — Inter (58 nodes), IBM Plex Sans (53, shipped by the grid library), Arial (30, unstyled native selects).
  • Line-height mostly normal; grid rows render 14px text on a 39px line box.

Here: Inter at 12/14/16/20, Space Grotesk at 24/30/36, Rubik uppercase for eyebrow labels. Seven sizes, all on scale, all with defined line-heights.

5 · Colour

The tool's neutral ramp is warm sepia — body #1A1612, secondary #3D3630, muted #7A7060, canvas #F8F6F1. The CTD system's Gray Modern ramp is cool (#121926 → #FCFCFD). Opposite temperature — most of why the tool doesn't read as CTD.

Also found: five stray blues (#1A1F5E, #2A3175, #2C4BB0, #2D35A0, #1A4FA8), none of them #18355E or Blue-600 #2868EE; a goldenrod #B7860B funnel bar in no palette; and a purple gradient on Propensity, which the system explicitly forbids.

6 · The four state columns

You said all four carry meaning, so all four are on the Accounts list — but with different visual weight and plain English, so the eye knows which to read first:

  • Signal (was Account State) — loudest. Semantic badge: Dancing, Re-triggered, Warm watch. This is what changed.
  • Funnel — neutral badge. Where the deal sits.
  • Work status (was Status) — quiet two-line text. in_progress (waiting_on) becomes "In progress / Waiting on reply"; done (cancelled) becomes "Cancelled / Closed by owner".
  • Strategy — quietest, an icon badge: Ready or Generating… It's the machine's state, not the account's.

A legend under the table defines Fit, Access, Signal, Work status and Rank — the old list showed a "Rank" of 9820 with no explanation anywhere.

7 · Path explorer (People)

"+101 more paths" was a dead end — CTD's core value, hidden behind a label. Expanding a person now ranks every path with the connector, the hop chain, the strength of the final hop, where CTD observed the relationship (LinkedIn, Gmail, internal), how recently, and a per-path Ask for intro. Sortable by strength, hops or recency.

A Your connectors table flips the view: who on your side can open a door, how many personas they reach, and how many intros they've actually made. That's what turns coverage into a decision.

8 · Coordination view (History)

The audit's sharpest finding was buried in prose: two reps hit different contacts with near-identical invites four days apart. By rep makes it structural — each rep's touches, contacts, and warm paths left unused, plus a coverage table showing that the only person who showed intent is the only one nobody targeted.

9 · Component discipline

  • Five tab patterns existed side by side. Now one Tabs component, with SegmentedControl for view switching inside a card.
  • Five button styles, including a pure-black "Add Input". Now three hierarchies from the system.
  • Emoji in navigation (📋 🔍 👀 ♟️ 📊 🟢 ⚠️) — the system's content rules say emoji are never used in product UI. Replaced with the 24px line-icon set.
  • Raw database values leaked into the UI: in_progress (waiting_on), none_identified, operating_company_v1, send_warm_i…. All rewritten in sentence case.
  • The unstyled data grid in Strategy → Tasks duplicated the global Tasks page and rendered a 400px empty void with no empty state. Removed; tasks live in one place.
  • Every empty state now says what to do next instead of "No inputs yet. Click Add Input to add one."

10 · Two structural fixes

  • Tab state isn't in the URL. Loading /strategy?tab=triggers lands on whatever tab React last held, so no view can be linked or shared. Every tab here should be a real route.
  • Account nav persists on global pages. Kestrel Dynamics's nine sub-items stayed visible on the Accounts list. Account scope now lives entirely in the page, under a breadcrumb.

11 · The Tasks workflow

The four things AEs get stuck on, and what the execute screen does about each:

  • "What step am I at?" — the chain (pick_play_path → execute_warm_intro → send_warm_intro) was only visible on a third tab called Activities, and two of the three steps shared the same title. It's now a stepper at the top of the task showing all four steps, who did each, when, and what they decided.
  • "Who is sending the email?" — the old screen stacked two different messages for three different people in one panel with no labels: your ask to the connector, and a pre-written note from the connector to the target. Each message is now its own card with an explicit From and To, and the second is visually marked as a handoff — dashed border, "Not sent by you".
  • "What does delivery method mean?" — Email vs CTD.ai was a bare radio pair that silently changed the primary button from "Send Email" to "Create & Share Ghost Mail". They're now labelled cards that state the consequence: sends from your inbox now, versus the connector approves it in CTD first.
  • Edit vs Preview — a global toggle that sat next to the delivery radio and applied ambiguously. It's now per-message, on the message it affects.

Two other changes. Send Email and Next task were both dark primaries — Next task is now secondary, so there's one obvious action. And the only exits were Send or Decline, with Decline buried under an unlabelled textarea; the action bar now offers Snooze, Save draft and Wrong connector, and states in words exactly what the primary will do.

11b · What happened to the task page's three tabs

Execute taskThe page itself
ActivitiesFolded into "The play" relay
Strategy briefSame name, collapsed in place

Activities was the only place the chain was visible, and it shared a name with the unrelated Activities concept on the account page.

It first became a four-step stepper, which then turned out to be redundant: a stepper, a "who sends what" relay and a delivery radio were three renderings of one thing. There are only ever two plays — you ask the connector yourself, or you write the ask and a more senior or better-connected colleague sends it under their name. So it is now a single The play card: pick the sender, see the relay, done. Three sections became one.

Strategy brief repeated the account's Verdict, Executive Summary, Strategic Goal and Why This Strategy — the eighth copy of the same narrative. Deleting the copy was right; deleting the AE's access to it mid-task was not. A panel keeping the same name now sits under the stepper with a one-paragraph summary always visible and the full reasoning one click away, split into why this person, why this connector, why now, and the risk. It links through to the account brief rather than restating it, so there is still one source.

12 · Simplified task taxonomy

Eight types, six statuses, and overlap between the two. on_hold was both a type and a status; manual_flow was a type while in_manual_flow was a status; send_warm_intro and execute_warm_intro read as synonyms but are different stages.

outreach_plan · pick_play_path · decide_next_step · execute_warm_introPath & draft
send_warm_intro · send_emailSend
manual_flowManual step
on_hold (as a type)Deleted — it's a status

Picking the connector and writing the first draft are one person's job, so they are one step — which drops the play from four CTD steps to two: path & draft, then send. The third step on the stepper is Nathan reaching Dean, which CTD tracks rather than assigns.

2nd degree only. Every path is you → one connector → the target, so hop counts are meaningless and have been removed everywhere. The People tab previously showed routes with two intermediaries — Alex Tanaka → Owen Brandt → Elena Roche — which the product cannot action. Those are now single-connector paths, and the Brief, the path explorer and Your connectors all name the same connector for a given target.

Statuses become Your turn · Waiting on connector · Snoozed · Done · Declined · Cancelled. in_manual_flow disappears, since the step already says so.

Worth noting against the Analytics tab: 516 tasks, 178 open, 100 cancelled, 25 overdue — and zero intros sent across every connector. A quarter of everything created gets cancelled. That's the number to watch after this ships.

13 · Meetings and Activities, folded in

Both carried real substance, so neither was cut — they were merged, which is what you get when you stop treating "a meeting" and "an interaction" as different objects.

Meetings → listHistory timeline, Meetings filter
Meetings → per-meeting summaryExpandable inside the timeline entry

Activities → Objections / BlockersBrief — What is blocking
Activities → Key signals / concernsBrief — Why drawers, Watch for

A meeting is now a timeline entry that opens into its summary, what is working, pain points and risks. Nothing is lost, and you stop having to reconcile two separate chronologies.

Deal assessment was six pink chips reading ACTIVE, ACCELERATING, HIGH, STRONG, IDENTIFIED_AND_ACTIVE, YES — colour that implied error where it meant "good". It is now six labelled cells with a status dot and a supporting fact, so Champion · Active · Marcus Webb reads as a sentence.

Blockers got promoted. Objections and blockers were the seventh block down on a tab nobody opened, yet they are the only thing standing between this deal and a pilot. They now sit directly under the deal assessment as three ordered gates with state — cleared, open, not started.

People adapts to the stage. On a cold account the table asks who can open a door. On a live deal it asks who blocks, who signs and who champions — same component, different column.

Human Inputs and Docs stay merged into History. Both were empty even on your most active account, which is the argument against giving either its own nav item.

14 · Tasks, in two places on purpose

The global Tasks queue answers "what should I do next across everything I own" — that is the morning workflow and it stays. The account's Tasks tab answers "what is in flight here" — that is the account-review workflow. Same records, two entry points, because those are two genuinely different jobs.

Scoped to one account, tasks are grouped by the play they belong to rather than listed flat, which is what the old Progress tab was reaching for. Each play shows its steps with state, who is holding it, and how long it has been sitting. A play waiting on the prospect looks different from one waiting on you.

That also absorbs the last orphan: Progress was a dense indented wall covering every account at once. Per-account it is legible, and the cross-account roll-up is what the Tasks queue already does.

15 · Timeline event types

The first pass invented labels ad hoc — "Engagement", "Outbound", "Product", "Origin" — and the two accounts did not even use the same set. Six types now, on one axis: who did it, and did we cause it.

MeetingA call — expands to its summary
EmailSent or received
SignalThey did something we did not cause
MilestoneA change in deal state
ExternalA market event about them
NoteRecorded by a person

Signal is the one that earns its place. A webinar attendance or three reads of the API docs is prospect-initiated intent — categorically different from an email we sent, and the thing an AE most wants to spot. "Engagement" blurred it with replies to our own outreach, which is exactly the mistake the Kestrel account was making.

A legend sits under every timeline, because six colour-coded chips with no key is how you get a vocabulary nobody learns. Note that play types on the Tasks tab — warm intro, intro, follow-up, pricing — are a separate vocabulary on purpose: one describes what happened, the other describes what you are trying to do.

16 · The four roles

A play has exactly four participants, and naming them fixed a run of problems that kept resurfacing as layout questions.

AuthorSDR or AE who found the path and wrote it
RequestorMakes the ask — has the relationship and the clout
SenderKnows the target and the requestor — makes the intro
TargetThe meeting you want

Two wrong turns got here. The ghost option first read "Marco sends it for you" — but Marco was the author, and an author never sends. The fix then invented a senior colleague as the sender, which put a stranger on the screen. Both were the same mistake: a name with no stated role.

The roles also explain what a ghost email is for. Author and requestor are usually the same person; they separate precisely when the connector would take the ask more seriously from someone senior. That is now the choice offered when creating a play, rather than a transport setting buried in a radio pair.

And the messages fall out of the model. Message 1 is requestor to sender and is the only one that gets sent from CTD. Message 2 is sender to target, written for the sender to use in their own words. The target never hears from CTD at all — which is the whole point of a warm intro, and was the single most confusing thing about the original screen.
